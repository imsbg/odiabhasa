The Sacred Flames of Odia Deepabali: Unraveling the Tradition of Badabadua Daka and Kaunria Badi

Across most of the Indian subcontinent, Deepabali is synonymous with sparkling fireworks, rows of earthen lamps, and prayers dedicated to Goddess Lakshmi. In Odisha, however, the festival carries a distinct, deeply emotional spiritual dimension. While celebrations and sweets abound, the true spiritual heart of Odia Deepabali beats for those who came before us.

On the moonless night of Kartika Amavasya, every Odia household performs Badabadua Daka (ବାଡ଼ବାଡ଼ୁଆ ଡାକ)—a timeless ritual that transforms Diwali into a bridge between the mortal realm and the realm of ancestors (Pitruloka).

The Belief: A Guiding Light Through the Cosmic Dark

The cycle of ancestral remembrance in Odia culture begins weeks earlier during Pitru Paksha, culminating on Mahalaya. According to belief, ancestral spirits (pitrus) leave their celestial abode and descend to earth to visit their descendants and bless their homes.

For an entire month, they remain near their kin. By the time Deepabali Amavasya arrives, it is time for them to embark on their journey back.

Because the night is dark and the cosmic path vast, the living light fiery beacons toward the heavens. The blazing flame does not just illuminate the night sky—it serves as a lighthouse guiding departed forefathers back to their heavenly resting place without losing their way.

The Sacred Medium: Kaunria Badi (କାଉଁରିଆ ବାଡ଼ି)

The core ritual instrument is the Kaunria Badi (or Kaunria Kathi)—the dried stem of the jute plant (Corchorus).

In traditional agrarian Odisha, jute harvest concludes shortly before the festival season. Once the fiber is extracted, the dried, pithy stalks are bundled together and tied securely with banana fibers or unbleached thread.

At dusk, courtyards and doorsteps are swept clean and decorated with intricate jhoti or chita (rice-paste floor art). Near the sacred Tulasi Chaura (holy basil altar), a brass lamp burning ghee is lit. The family gathers around, lights the tips of their Kaunria bundles from the sacred flame, and points the burning stalks upward into the sky.

As the sparks crackle and the distinctive aromatic white smoke drifts heavenward, every generation—from the oldest patriarch to the youngest child—holds the burning stalks and chants together.

The Invocation: The Badabadua Chant

The words uttered during the ritual are ancient, evocative, and deeply rooted in Jagannath consciousness:

"ବଡ଼ବଡୁଆ ହୋ,
ଅନ୍ଧାରରେ ଆସ, ଆଲୁଅରେ ଯାଅ,
ଗଙ୍ଗା ଯାଅ, ଗୟା ଯାଅ,
ବାଇଶି ପାହାଚେ ଗଡ଼ଗଡ଼ାଉ ଥାଅ।"
  • "ବଡ଼ବଡୁଆ ହୋ" (Badabadua ho): An affectionate, reverential call invoking the departed elders, ancestors, and forefathers across generations.
  • "ଅନ୍ଧାରରେ ଆସ, ଆଲୁଅରେ ଯାଅ" (Andhaara re aasa, aalua re jaa'a): "You arrived under the shroud of darkness during Mahalaya; return now along this sacred path of radiant light."
  • "ଗଙ୍ଗା ଯାଅ, ଗୟା ଯାଅ" (Ganga jaa'a, Gaya jaa'a): An earnest wish that the souls attain holy passage and spiritual purification at the sacred pilgrimage waters of the Ganga and the sanctified plains of Gaya.
  • "ବାଇଶି ପାହାଚେ ଗଡ଼ଗଡ଼ାଉ ଥାଅ" (Baishi Pahache gada gadaa'u thaa'a): The ultimate blessing in Odia spiritual philosophy. The chant wishes that the ancestors reach the holy abode of Lord Jagannath in Puri and attain ultimate liberation (moksha) by rolling on the sacred 22 steps (Baishi Pahacha) inside Srimandir.

The Grand Spectacle at Puri Bada Danda

While this ritual is performed at every household's doorstep across rural and urban Odisha, its most breathtaking manifestation happens on the Grand Road (Bada Danda) right before the Singhadwara (Lion's Gate) of Srimandir in Puri.

Tens of thousands of pilgrims assemble in front of the temple carrying bundles of Kaunria Kathi. As darkness descends, the entire expanse of Bada Danda turns into a sea of undulating fire and swirling fragrant smoke. The rhythmic chanting of "Badabadua ho..." reverberates against the towering walls of the temple, creating an unforgettable atmosphere of raw devotion, ancestral gratitude, and community bonding.

After the ritual, families offer Paya Shradha, light traditional clay lamps around their houses, and share authentic festive delicacies like Arisa Pitha, Kakara, and Manda.

Frequently Asked Questions: Odia Deepabali & Badabadua Daka

Badabadua Daka (ବାଡ଼ବାଡ଼ୁଆ ଡାକ) is an ancient ancestral invocation performed across Odisha on Deepabali evening (Kartika Amavasya). Families light bundles of dried jute sticks (Kaunria Kathi) and hold them skyward to show light to their departed ancestors (pitrus), guiding them back to their heavenly abode (Pitruloka).

According to Odia belief, ancestors descend to earth to visit their descendants during Pitru Paksha, beginning on Mahalaya. They stay among the living for a month. Deepabali Amavasya marks the conclusion of their stay. Lighting torches on this moonless night ensures their journey back through the cosmic darkness is brightly illuminated and filled with blessings.

Kaunria Badi refers to the dried inner pith/stalk of the jute plant (Corchorus). After the jute fiber is stripped during the autumn harvest, these lightweight, flammable white sticks are dried in the sun and bound into bundles using natural fibers like banana bark or cotton twine. They produce a bright golden flame and a distinctive, aromatic smoke considered sacred for ritual invocations.

The traditional verse recited is:
"ବଡ଼ବଡୁଆ ହୋ, ଅନ୍ଧାରରେ ଆସ, ଆଲୁଅରେ ଯାଅ, ଗଙ୍ଗା ଯାଅ, ଗୟା ଯାଅ, ବାଇଶି ପାହାଚେ ଗଡ଼ଗଡ଼ାଉ ଥାଅ।"

  • ବଡ଼ବଡୁଆ ହୋ (Badabadua ho): Reverently addressing the ancestral elders.
  • ଅନ୍ଧାରରେ ଆସ, ଆଲୁଅରେ ଯାଅ (Andhaara re aasa, aalua re jaa'a): "You came during the dark fortnight of Mahalaya; now return along this path of light."
  • ଗଙ୍ଗା ଯାଅ, ଗୟା ଯାଅ (Ganga jaa'a, Gaya jaa'a): Wishing the departed souls holy passage through the sacred pilgrimage realms of the Ganga and Gaya.
  • ବାଇଶି ପାହାଚେ ଗଡ଼ଗଡ଼ାଉ ଥାଅ (Baishi Pahache gada gadaa'u thaa'a): Invoking the ultimate spiritual liberation (moksha) by wishing their souls to roll on the holy 22 steps (Baishi Pahacha) of the Jagannath Temple in Puri.

In Jagannath theology, the 22 steps leading from the Singhadwara up to the inner courtyard are spiritually purifying. It is believed that anyone whose touch or memory connects with these steps attains deliverance from the cycle of rebirth. Praying for ancestors to reach the Baishi Pahacha represents the highest wish for their eternal salvation.

The ritual is held strictly at dusk on Deepabali Amavasya (around Sandhya vela / twilight). Families clean their front courtyard or threshold, draw jhoti (rice paste art), light an earthen ghee lamp near the Tulasi Chaura, and then ignite the Kaunria bundles from that sacred flame.

Tens of thousands of devotees gather outside the Singhadwara on Bada Danda in Puri to perform Badabadua Daka directly facing Lord Jagannath's temple. The sight of thousands of Kaunria torches burning simultaneously against the night sky makes it one of the most culturally significant mass rituals in Odisha.

Families prepare traditional Odia pithas such as Arisa Pitha, Kakara, Manda Pitha, along with seasonal fruits and chhena (cottage cheese sweets) and more.

Keeping Our Heritage and Language Alive for the Next Generation

Festivals like Deepabali and rituals like Badabadua Daka carry centuries of literature, philosophy, and cultural identity. For our children to appreciate why we chant these verses and what connects us to our roots, learning our mother tongue is the very first step.

When children can read Odia fluently, cultural traditions cease to be mere mechanical rituals—they become stories of pride, identity, and shared heritage.
